Level 33 — Confounding & The FEV Paradox (the moment your stomach drops) +60 XP
Business Story — Boston, 1970s

Researchers measured Forced Expiratory Volume (FEV, lung function) in 654 children ages 3–19 at a Boston hospital. Each child was coded as smoker (1) or non-smoker (0). The naive question: does smoking hurt lung function? They run a two-sample t-test on FEV by smoke. The result is shocking: smokers have HIGHER mean FEV than non-smokers. Wait — what?

R Code — the naive (wrong) analysis
FEV <- read_delim("FEV.txt", delim="\t")
t.test(fev ~ smoke, data=FEV)
# mean in non-smokers ≈ 2.57 L
# mean in smokers     ≈ 3.28 L  ← higher!?
# p-value < 0.001  → "statistically significant" in the WRONG direction
The Resolution: Age Is a Lurking Confounder

What predicts whether a child smokes? Age. A 4-year-old does not smoke; a 17-year-old might. What also predicts FEV? Age — older kids have bigger lungs. So "smokers" in this dataset are systematically older, and older kids have bigger lungs regardless of smoking. Age is a confounder: a third variable that influences both the predictor (smoke) and the outcome (FEV).

Analogy — Ice Cream and Drowning

"Ice cream sales" and "drowning deaths" are highly correlated across months. Does ice cream cause drowning? No — both are driven by summer. Smoking and FEV are correlated across kids — but both are driven by age. The fix is the same: hold the lurker constant.

R Code — the FIX: adjust for age

# Now the smoke coefficient flips:
#               Estimate  Std. Error  t value  Pr(>|t|)
# (Intercept)    0.367     0.081       4.51    7e-06
# smoke         -0.209     0.081      -2.59    0.010
# age            0.231     0.008      28.2     <2e-16
fev ~ smoke + age — the + means "include both predictors." Each coefficient is now the effect of its variable holding the others constant.
The smoke estimate is now −0.21 L: smoking is associated with a 0.21-liter decrease in FEV when comparing kids of the same age.
kable() from knitr just makes the output table render nicely in your knitted PDF.
Multiple Linear Regression Model

$$\widehat{y} = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_1 + \hat\beta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \hat\beta_p x_p$$

Each \(\hat\beta_j\) = "estimated change in y per +1 unit of \(x_j\), with all other \(x\)'s held constant." That phrase is the entire reason MLR exists. Memorize it.

⚠️ Adjustment Is Not Causation

Even after adjusting for age, this is observational data. We say smoking is "associated with" a decrease in FEV, NOT "smoking causes a decrease in FEV." Random assignment (Lec 6) is what licenses causal language; statistical adjustment merely controls for measured confounders — there could be unmeasured ones.

Level 34 — Multiple Linear Regression: interpreting coefficients +50 XP
Each Coefficient Is a Conditional Effect

In SLR, β̂₁ = "effect of x on y." In MLR, \(\hat\beta_j\) = "effect of \(x_j\) on y, holding all other predictors fixed." That conditional/partial nature is the magic — it's how we adjust for confounders.

Writing a Complete Conclusion (per Lec 8 conventions)

"We find statistically significant evidence at the α=0.05 level that smoking status is associated with lung function, while adjusting for age (p=0.010). Specifically, we estimate that smoking is associated with an average decrease in FEV of 0.21 liters when comparing children of the same age, with 95% CI: (−0.37, −0.05)."

  • "statistically significant evidence at the α=0.05 level" — never "proves" or "demonstrates"
  • "associated with" — never "causes" without random assignment
  • "while adjusting for age" — make explicit what was held constant
  • "when comparing children of the same age" — operationalize the conditional
  • Pair the point estimate with a 95% CI (use confint(model))

Analogy — Adjusting for a Variable Is Like Splitting the Group

Imagine binning children into age groups (3–6, 7–10, 11–14, 15–18) and computing a separate smoke vs. FEV difference within each bin, then averaging those differences. That's morally what lm(fev ~ smoke + age) is doing, just with continuous age and a clever weighting. "Compare apples to apples."

Level 35 — t-Test = lm with a binary predictor (the unification) +45 XP
The "Aha" Moment

You spent three lectures learning about the t-test. Then you spent two more learning about linear regression. Surprise: they are the same thing. A two-sample t-test (with var.equal=TRUE) is mathematically identical to fitting lm(y ~ group) where group is a binary 0/1 variable. The slope is the difference of means. The slope's t-statistic is the t-test statistic. Same p-value.

Why It Works

Code group as \(x \in \{0, 1\}\). Fit \(\hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x\).

  • When \(x=0\): \(\hat y = \hat\beta_0\) → that's the mean of group 0.
  • When \(x=1\): \(\hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1\) → that's the mean of group 1.
  • So \(\hat\beta_1\) = (mean of group 1) − (mean of group 0).

Test \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) ↔ test \(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2\). Same null. Same math.

R Code — Lec 8 demonstrated the equivalence
model1 <- lm(fev ~ smoke, data=FEV)
summary(model1)$coefficients[2,4]
# [1] 5.46e-13

t.test(fev ~ smoke, data=FEV, var.equal=TRUE)$p.value
# [1] 5.46e-13   ← IDENTICAL

# Welch's (unequal variances) is slightly different — different model
t.test(fev ~ smoke, data=FEV)$p.value
# [1] ~7e-09
Analogy — Two Scripts, One Movie

The t-test and lm are like watching the same movie with two different captions on. Same scenes happening, same plot — the captions just emphasize different things. The t-test caption says "test of means." The lm caption says "test of a slope." But the math doing the work underneath is one engine.

Why This Matters

Because the t-test is just a special case of lm, the SAME framework handles ALL the harder problems: more than two groups (use a categorical predictor), continuous predictors, multiple predictors, interactions. The t-test was a stepping stone. From here on, it's all lm.

Quiz
If t-test ≡ lm, why does Lec 8 show that t.test() and summary(lm) p-values don't quite match in some examples?
It's a numerical bug in R.
The t-test uses a different distribution.
R's default t.test() uses Welch's correction (unequal variances). lm assumes equal variances. Add var.equal=TRUE to t.test and they match exactly.
Because lm uses different rounding.
Level 36 — Penguins & Simpson's Paradox (the sign flips!) +60 XP
Business Story — Palmer Station, Antarctica

An ecologist measures bill length and bill depth on n=344 penguins. She runs lm(bill_depth_mm ~ bill_length_mm) and gets a NEGATIVE slope: longer bills → shallower depths. Conservation biologists are confused — that contradicts every species' developmental biology. What gives? Species is the lurking confounder. Within EACH species (Adelie, Chinstrap, Gentoo), the relationship is positive. Pooled across species, it's negative. This is Simpson's Paradox.

Simpson's Paradox in One Sentence

An association can have one direction in the marginal data and the opposite direction within every subgroup of a third variable. Aggregating across the third variable (species, age, etc.) without controlling for it can flip your conclusion.

🎮 Interactive — Penguin Sign Flip

Below: simulated penguin-style data (3 species, n=120). Toggle "Color by Species" to see why the sign flips. Toggle the two regression lines.

Marginal slope (no species): NEGATIVE (≈ −0.08)
Within-species slopes: ALL POSITIVE (≈ +0.18 to +0.25)
R Code — penguin paradox in 3 lines
library(palmerpenguins)
summary(lm(bill_depth_mm ~ bill_length_mm, data=penguins))$coefficients
#                  Estimate
# bill_length_mm   -0.085   ← NEGATIVE

summary(lm(bill_depth_mm ~ bill_length_mm + species, data=penguins))$coefficients
#                  Estimate
# bill_length_mm    0.200   ← POSITIVE — sign flipped!
Adding + species tells lm to fit separate intercepts per species (since species is a categorical/factor variable). The bill_length slope is then the within-species slope — the apples-to-apples comparison.
If you forget to adjust for species, you "see" a negative slope because Gentoo (long bills, shallow) sits in the upper-left and Chinstrap/Adelie (short bills, deep) sit in the lower-right of the cloud.
Analogy — UC Berkeley Admissions (1973)

Famous case: Berkeley graduate admissions appeared biased against women in aggregate (44% of men admitted, 35% of women). But within almost every department, women had EQUAL or HIGHER admission rates. Why the flip? Women applied disproportionately to highly competitive departments with low admission rates for everyone. Department was the confounder. Same shape as the penguin example.

Level 37 — Categorical Predictors with k>2 levels: dummy coding +50 XP
Business Story — Therapy Dogs Study

A mental health study has 3 treatment groups: Control (no dog), Indirect (dog in room, can't pet), Direct (can pet the dog). Outcome: post-pre change in loneliness score (negative = improved). You want to know "do groups differ?" — but Group has 3 levels, not 2. How does lm() handle that?

Dummy / Indicator Coding

R picks one level alphabetically as the reference (here: Control). It then creates k−1 binary "dummy" variables for the remaining levels. The model becomes:

$$\widehat{\text{diff}} = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 \cdot \mathbb{1}[\text{Direct}] + \hat\beta_2 \cdot \mathbb{1}[\text{Indirect}]$$

  • Control: \(\hat\beta_0\) alone
  • Direct: \(\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1\)
  • Indirect: \(\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_2\)

So \(\hat\beta_1\) = (mean of Direct) − (mean of Control), and \(\hat\beta_2\) = (mean of Indirect) − (mean of Control). Each coefficient is a difference from the reference.

R Code — therapy dogs
lonely <- read_csv("dog_data_lonely.csv")
groups_model <- lm(diff ~ Group, data=lonely)
summary(groups_model)

# Coefficients (note "Group" reference is Control):
#                Estimate  Pr(>|t|)
# (Intercept)     -0.30     0.39    ← Control mean diff
# GroupDirect     -2.85     0.001   ← Direct - Control
# GroupIndirect   -1.90     0.02    ← Indirect - Control
R automatically created GroupDirect and GroupIndirect as 0/1 dummies. Control is the reference (its dummy = both zeros).
The intercept is no longer "y when x=0" in a continuous sense — it's the mean for the reference category.
Each row's t-test asks: "Is this group's mean different from the reference's mean?"
⚠️ Per-row p-values do NOT answer "do groups differ?"

The two t-tests above answer "is Direct different from Control?" and "is Indirect different from Control?" — separately. They do NOT answer the omnibus question "is there ANY difference among the three groups?" Worse, doing two t-tests inflates Type I error. To test the omnibus null, you need the partial F-test in Level 38.

Analogy — The Reference Pole

Imagine planting a flag in Control's mean and measuring how far each other group's mean is from that flag. β̂₁ and β̂₂ are those distances. To ask "are any of the flags far from each other?" you need a different question — that's the F-test.

Level 38 — Partial F-Test: testing groups of coefficients (with RSS waterfall) +70 XP
The Question the Partial F-Test Answers

"Does adding this group of predictors significantly improve the model, beyond a smaller model?" For the dogs study, "is there ANY difference among the three groups?" maps to comparing:

  • null model: diff ~ 1 (intercept only — predicts the overall mean)
  • full model: diff ~ Group (separate mean per group)

The omnibus null is \(H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = 0\) (both Direct AND Indirect dummies are zero). If we reject, at least one group differs from Control.

The F-Statistic

$$\mathcal{F} = \frac{(RSS_{\text{null}} - RSS_{\text{full}})\,/\,p}{RSS_{\text{full}}\,/\,(n-k)}$$

  • \(n\) = total sample size
  • \(k\) = number of coefficients in the full model
  • \(p\) = number of EXTRA coefficients in the full model (vs the null) = degrees-of-freedom difference
  • \(RSS_{\text{null}} - RSS_{\text{full}}\) = how much the bigger model explains
  • \(RSS_{\text{full}}/(n-k)\) = leftover noise per residual df

Under H₀, \(\mathcal{F}\) follows an F-distribution with \((p, n-k)\) degrees of freedom. Big F → small p → reject.

🎮 Interactive — RSS Waterfall (the F-test, visualized)

Drag the slider for "true between-group spread" to see how it drives the F-statistic. When groups have similar means, the full model barely beats the null → small F → big p. As between-group spread grows, the full model wins big → F skyrockets → tiny p.

R Code — partial F-test for the therapy dogs
null_model  <- lm(diff ~ 1,    data=lonely)
groups_model <- lm(diff ~ Group, data=lonely)

anova(null_model, groups_model)
# Analysis of Variance Table
#
# Model 1: diff ~ 1
# Model 2: diff ~ Group
#   Res.Df    RSS  Df  Sum of Sq      F    Pr(>F)
# 1    283   1234
# 2    281   1078   2     156      20.3   2.4e-09  ← omnibus p-value

# Manually:
RSS_null <- sum((lonely$diff - mean(lonely$diff))^2)
RSS_full <- sum(groups_model$residuals^2)
F_stat <- ((RSS_null - RSS_full) / 2) /
          (RSS_full / (nrow(lonely) - 3))

Analogy — The Lawyer's Question

The partial F-test is the lawyer's question: "Counselor, did your additional witnesses (the Group dummies) significantly reduce the unexplained variance compared to no witnesses at all?" The numerator is the explained variance reduction. The denominator is the noise floor (variance left over per residual df). If the reduction is much larger than baseline noise, F is huge and the judge (your α) accepts the witnesses as relevant.
